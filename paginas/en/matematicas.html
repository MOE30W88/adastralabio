Astronomical clock

The sky, time and the Sun's position in a single sphere.

Every piece of the clock answers to a formula. Here we explain what it calculates, where it comes from and how it would look with real numbers, in the same order as the layers of the Astrolabe. The diagrams are illustrations; the numerical examples are calculated with the project's code.

Common convention: angles are measured from the top, clockwise, and time is read on a 24-hour dial. The precision of each calculation is detailed in Scope.

Clock

How time becomes angles, and the time of day becomes a point in the drawing.

Clock

Clock background

The clock is drawn on a plane whose center is the point (600, 600). Everything on it, marks, hands and planets, is placed with polar coordinates: a distance r from the center and an angle θ.

Unlike the usual mathematical convention, the angle is measured from the top, clockwise, as on a clock. That choice makes 0° coincide with 12 h on the dial. The reading ring (radius 665) is a supporting circle that gives contrast to the scale numbers when the Clock layer is in focus.

For the reading ring, r = 665. At θ = 90° (to the right of the dial): x = 600 + 665·sin 90° = 1265 and y = 600 − 665·cos 90° = 600. The point lies to the right of the center and at the same height.

Clock

Frame and hour scale

The dial has 24 divisions, not 12: the hand makes one turn per day. Noon is at the top (0°) and midnight at the bottom (±180°). Each hour spans 360° ÷ 24 = 15°.

The marks at 0, 6, 12 and 18 h are longer (cardinal). The Roman numerals repeat from I to XII twice: the index is obtained with the remainder of division by 12.

h = 18 → θ = 15·18 − 180 = 90° (to the right). h = 6 → θ = −90° (to the left). h = 15 → θ = 45°. Numeral: h = 13 → k = (13 + 11) mod 12 = 0 → “I”; h = 12 → k = 11 → “XII”.

Drawing measurements: the cardinal marks run from r = 660 to 680, the rest from 660 to 670, and the numerals are placed at r = 705.

Clock

Minute scale

Around the hour dial runs a scale of 60 marks, one per minute. A full turn equals 60 min, so each minute spans 360° ÷ 60 = 6°. The marks that are multiples of 5 are longer and carry a number; mark 0 is labeled 60.

Clock

Hour hand

The hour hand makes one turn per day. To place it, the minutes elapsed since local midnight are counted, including seconds, and the 1440 minutes of the day are spread over 360°: 360° ÷ 1440 min = 0.25°/min, that is, 1/4 of a degree per minute (15° per hour).

The wall-clock time of the chosen place is used (the applied time zone), not universal time. The hand changes color when the Sun is below the horizon.

Numerical example

At 15:30:00: M = 60·15 + 30 = 930 → θ = 930/4 − 180 = 52.5°. Midnight: M = 0 → −180° (bottom). Noon: M = 720 → 0° (top). An ordinary 12 h clock would give (930 mod 720)/2 = 105°, because its hand turns twice as fast.

Clock

Minute hand

The minute hand sweeps 360° in an hour: 6° per minute. The fraction of a minute contributed by the seconds and milliseconds is added, so the motion is continuous and does not advance in jumps.

Since the hour hand makes 1 turn per day and the minute hand 24, the ratio between them is 24 to 1 (on a 12 h clock it is 12 to 1).

Clock

Second hand

The second hand makes one turn every 60 s, 6° per second, and adds the fraction in milliseconds to move smoothly. It uses the same instant as the other hands: if the clock's time is sped up or stopped, the second hand follows.

Clock

Clock markers

On the edge of the scale there are three small triangles that accompany each hand and point to its reading on the scale: one for the hour, one for the minute and one for the second. Each triangle is defined with three points in polar coordinates: the tip, resting on the edge of the dial (r = 600), and two base vertices at a larger radius and at ±α from the axis.

Then the whole triangle is rotated by the same angle as the corresponding hand (θH, θM, θS). The diagram exaggerates α so it can be seen.

Clock

Analemma

If the Sun's position is recorded at the same clock time over a year, it draws an elongated figure eight: the analemma. Its height is the Sun's declination (δ) and its width is the equation of time (E): the difference between the time shown by the Sun and that of a uniform clock.

Two causes produce it: the Earth's orbit is elliptical (the Earth moves faster at perihelion) and the axis is tilted ε = 23.44°. The AD ASTRA clock shows solar time and civil time at the sides; their difference is E.

Numerical example

3 November 2026 at 12:00 mean solar time in San Salvador (−89.19°): λ☉ = 221.32°, δ = −15.23° (check: sin 23.44°·sin 221.32° = −0.263 → −15.23°) and E = +16.45 min. In the drawing: x = 600 + (16.45/16)·342 ≈ 952 and y = 590 + (15.23/23.44)·500 ≈ 915.

The divisor 16 on the horizontal axis is a nominal value: in November E reaches 16.45 min, so the point lies just outside the nominal ellipse. Precision of these quantities: declination to 0.016° and E to about 4 s (Scope).

Day and night

How the Sun and the Moon become light, color and marks on the dial.

Day and night

Solar events

The outer ring of the dial is a 24 h clock painted with the color of the sky. It is divided into 720 sectors of 2 minutes; each is colored according to the Sun's altitude at that time at the chosen place. Thus the ring shows at a glance when it is night, when twilight and when day.

The color is not calculated with atmospheric optics: it is an artistic scale of stops by altitude, between which it interpolates linearly.

With the Sun at h = −9°: it lies between the stops −12° (44, 26, 72) and −8° (106, 44, 78), so t = (−9 + 12)/(−8 + 12) = 0.75 and the color is (44 + 0.75·62, 26 + 0.75·18, 72 + 0.75·6) ≈ (91, 40, 77), a reddish violet. The altitudes that separate the sky bands are those of the twilights: civil (−6°), nautical (−12°) and astronomical (−18°). Sunrise and sunset are defined at −0.833° (see Horizon).

Day and night

Sun

The Sun is drawn on the dial at the time the clock shows (the same position as the hour hand), with a light that depends on its altitude above the place's horizon: invisible below the horizon, faint at dawn and full from 15° of altitude.

To obtain the altitude the Sun's position in the sky is needed. The project uses a simple approximation of the solar position: three lines of formulas calculate its longitude, its declination and its right ascension.

Day and night

Moon

The Moon is placed on the same dial as the Sun. Its position depends on how far it is from the Sun in the sky: at new moon it is next to it, at full moon on the opposite side of the dial, and at the quarters a quarter turn away.

Its light depends on two things: that it is above the horizon and that the sky is dark enough. By day it does not disappear completely (15 % remains), but it looks very faint.

12 October 2026, 18:00 local (24:00 UTC), San Salvador: α☉ = 198.16° and α☾ = 224.08°, so α☉ − α☾ = −25.93°. With θH = 90°: θ☾ = 90 − 25.93 = 64.07°. The Moon is at 14.2° altitude and the Sun at −5.9°: f = 1, IS = 0, v = 1 and therefore I☾ = 1.

This lunar position is the low-precision one: it can be off in right ascension by up to about 2.7° and does not correct parallax, so it serves to place the Moon on the dial, not to predict rises or occultations (Scope). Phases and eclipses use the high-precision model.

Day and night

Planets above the horizon

Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are drawn on the outer edge of the day and night ring, each with its name. Their position on the dial follows the same rule as the Moon: the Sun's angle plus the difference in right ascension, that is, where the planet is relative to the Sun and not at what altitude it is seen in the sky.

A planet is considered visible to the naked eye only if it meets three conditions: being at least 3° above the horizon, being at least 8° away from the Sun, and being brighter than the limiting magnitude the sky allows at that moment (the darker it is, the fainter the stars that can be seen). Those that do not meet them are not drawn; the visible ones shine more the greater their margin over that limit, so they appear gradually as it gets dark and fade out at dawn.

Day and night

Eclipse

An eclipse is a geometry problem between circles. In a solar eclipse, the Moon covers part of the Sun's disk as seen from your position: the sizes of the two disks and how far apart their centers are are compared. In a lunar one, the Moon enters the Earth's shadow: its radius is compared with that of the umbra at that distance.

When the eclipse occurs, the Eclipse layer of the clock draws the disk in progress, with the relative size and position that come out of these quantities.

Numerical example

Total lunar eclipse of 3 March 2026, 11:33:49 UTC: Ru = 4674.2 km, rL = 1738.1 km and d = 2402.5 km, whence mu = (4674.2 + 1738.1 − 2402.5)/(2·1738.1) = 1.153. Since mu ≥ 1 the entire disk is inside the umbra: total.

Solar eclipse of 12 August 2026, in Reykjavik, at 17:45:43 UTC (global maximum): ρ☉ = 0.263°, ρ☾ = 0.273° and s = 0.027°, whence mS = 0.968 and O = 96.9 %. Since s is greater than ρ☾ − ρ☉ = 0.010°, the Sun is not completely covered at that instant: partial.

How it is classified: total solar if ρ☾ ≥ ρ☉ and s ≤ |ρ☉ − ρ☾|; annular if ρ☾ < ρ☉ with that same condition; otherwise partial. Total lunar if mu ≥ 1, partial if 0 < mu < 1 and penumbral if there is only penumbral magnitude. The global dates and classes of the 2026–2030 eclipses match NASA's; local circumstances (such as the Reykjavik example) have not been validated (Scope).

Day and night

Horizon

The horizon is the line that separates the visible sky from the part beneath your feet. In the drawing it is the horizontal line passing through the center of the clock, with the observer standing on it.

Sunrise and sunset do not occur when the Sun's center crosses the geometric horizon. A somewhat lower threshold is used: the upper edge of the disk appears (or disappears) when the Sun's center is about 0.833° below, because refraction in the atmosphere raises the Sun's image by about 34′ and the solar disk has a radius of 16′.

Numerical example

San Salvador (13.69° N), 12 October 2026: δ = −7.60° and cos H0 = 0.0174, whence H0 = 89.00° and T = 11.867 h (≈ 11 h 52 min). With the minute-by-minute sweep that the clock uses, the result is 11.865 h. On 21 June: δ = +23.44° → H0 = 97.00° and T = 12.934 h (sweep: 12.936 h).

The clock does not use the closed formula: it calculates the Sun's altitude every minute for 24 h and looks for the crossings with linear interpolation. If the Sun stays above h0 all day, “midnight sun” is reported; if it never reaches h0, “polar night”. Real refraction changes with pressure and temperature, and the observer's elevation is not included (Scope). The line in the drawing is a reference, not the mathematical horizon: the Sun is on it at 6 h and 18 h only approximately.

Event marks

Around the day and night ring nine marks appear with their time: the three phases of morning twilight, sunrise, solar noon, sunset and the three phases of evening twilight. Each mark is at the angle of the ring where the color changes, because they share the same 24 h scale.

The times come from the same minute-by-minute sweep as the Horizon, repeated with the altitude thresholds of each twilight: −0.833° (sunrise and sunset), −6° (civil), −12° (nautical) and −18° (astronomical). Solar noon is the minute of maximum altitude.

Lunarium

How the illuminated part of the Moon is drawn.

Lunarium

Lunar phase

The Moon at the center of the clock shows the illuminated part of its disk. What is seen depends on a single angle: how far the Moon is from the Sun in the sky (the elongation ψ). From it the phase angle i (the one formed by the Sun and Earth as seen from the Moon) is found, and from that the illuminated fraction k.

The drawing is a light and a shadow separated by a curve. The outer edge of the illuminated part is a semicircle (the limb); the inner edge, the terminator, is a semi-ellipse of the same height as the disk and of width R·|1 − 2k|. With small k the terminator is almost as wide as the disk (a sliver); with k = 0.5 the width is zero (quarter) and with k close to 1 it widens again (gibbous). By choosing the direction of each arc, the light lies on the right if the Moon is waxing and on the left if it is waning.

Numerical example

12 October 2026, 16:00 UTC: ψ = 24.13°. Since R is about 390 times greater than Δ, the phase angle is almost 180° − ψ ≈ 155.9° and k = (1 + cos 155.9°)/2 ≈ 0.044 (4 %). When there is a solar eclipse the Moon stops being drawn (the Sun covers it), and in a lunar eclipse the illuminated part changes from bluish gray to reddish by blending the two colors with a smooth curve depending on how far it enters the umbra.

Zodiac

How the Sun's path along the ecliptic becomes a rotating ring, an indicator and four references.

Zodiac

Zodiac band

The zodiac is the Sun's apparent path through the year, divided into 12 signs of 30° of ecliptic longitude (λ), counted from the March equinox. On the clock it is a ring of 12 sectors that rotates around the Sun as the year goes by.

Two details make it different from an ordinary disk. First, the ring is not centered on the Sun: its center is at (600; 463.4), about 136.6 units higher, and its radius is 343.4. Thus the edge of the ring reaches 480 from the Sun (the edge of the dial) exactly when its highest part is at the top, and 206.8 when it is at the bottom. Second, the signs are written in the opposite direction to longitude: inside the ring, the local angle grows clockwise while λ grows counterclockwise.

The whole ring is rotated by an angle A that depends only on the Sun's longitude. With that rotation, the longitude λ of any point lies at an angle (λ☉ − λ) from the vertical measured from the ring's center; in particular the Sun lies right under the reading axis, at the top.

Seasonal references

Four circles centered on the Sun mark the equinoxes and solstices on the dial. Each has as its radius the distance at which the zodiac ring crosses the vertical axis when the Sun is exactly at that longitude (0°, 90°, 180° or 270°).

That distance comes from a triangle. With the ring rotated by an angle A, its center lies at a distance e = 136.6 from the Sun in direction A. The point of the ring that falls on the vertical is at a distance ρ from the Sun such that, from the ring's center, it measures exactly its radius. Solving that condition (a quadratic equation in ρ) gives the formula below.

Since A depends only on λ, the four radii are constant: they do not change with the date or the place. What changes is the ring, which rotates; each time a sign crosses one of those circles, the Sun is passing through an equinox or a solstice.

Seasonal marker

When the Sun is at an equinox or solstice, a dot with the name of the event appears on the reading axis. The criterion is simple: the Sun's ecliptic longitude must be within 0.5° of 0°, 90°, 180° or 270°.

Since the Sun advances about 0.9856° per day, 0.5° equals half a day on each side: the marker stays lit for about a whole day around the exact instant. It does not indicate the instant of the event (that is calculated separately, with the ephemeris), it only signals that it is the day.

The dot is placed on the vertical axis at the distance ρ from the formula in Seasonal references, calculated with the current rotation: it therefore coincides with the circle of that reference. The label is written at the center of the rotated ring.

Tropics

Two circles concentric with the Sun mark, on the dial, the altitude the Sun reaches at noon at the solstices for your latitude. The same scale as the rest of the clock is used: the radius grows with altitude, from 0 (Sun at the nadir, −90°) to 480 (Sun at the zenith, +90°).

At the June solstice δ = +23.44° (Tropic of Cancer) and at the December one δ = −23.44° (Tropic of Capricorn); hence the names of the circles.

How the year is divided in a ring, and how the seasons and the Chinese year are read.

Calendar

Calendar

The outer ring of the clock is a circular calendar: the whole year, spread over 360°, with one mark per day. Each day spans 360° ÷ 365 ≈ 0.986° (or 360° ÷ 366 in a leap year), and each month an arc proportional to its number of days: February is the shortest, July and August the longest together.

The months begin where the days of the previous ones have accumulated. The month marks are longer, those of days 1, 5, 10, 15… carry a number on the outer edge and the rest are short marks. The ring rotates (see Date marker) and 1 January is at the top only when the year has just begun.

Date marker

A small fixed triangle, at the top, points to the current date. What moves is the calendar: it rotates against the passage of time so that, at every instant, the current day lies under the marker. The rotation angle is the one needed to bring to the top the position of the year already elapsed.

The day of the year is taken as a fraction (it includes the hours of the day, with the place's wall-clock time), so the ring moves continuously, not in jumps. The marker's tip is 4 units from the longest month mark (r = 804) and its base, at r = 822, spans ±0.8°: about 23 units wide, equivalent to about 1.6 days.

Calendar

Chinese calendar

The Chinese zodiac wheel has 12 sectors of 30°, one per animal, and a fixed marker at the top. Each Chinese year corresponds to an animal, in a 12-year cycle, and the wheel rotates so that the marker travels through the sector of the current animal from its starting edge to the end over the course of the Chinese year. When the next New Year arrives, the marker moves to the edge of the next sector.

The Chinese year does not begin on 1 January, but at the second new moon after the December solstice (the third if, between two solstices, the intercalary month falls right after month 11 or after month 12). To know how far the year has advanced, the instants of the new moons are calculated with a series of terms (Meeus), the rule of the months is applied, and it is measured in Beijing time (UTC+8). The numbers on the three outer rings are the years of each animal in the current cycle and in the two previous ones.

Solar system

How the orbits and the planets are calculated and drawn.

Solar system

Earth's orbit

The Earth travels an ellipse with the Sun at one of its foci. To know where it is on a date, we start from six orbital elements (size a, shape e, inclination I, orientation w and Ω and mean position L), whose values change very slowly with time T, measured in centuries since the year 2000.

The position comes from Kepler's equation, which links time with the angle traveled: the mean anomaly M (proportional to time) is calculated and E − e sin E = M is solved by iteration. With E the coordinates in the orbital plane are obtained, which are rotated to the ecliptic plane and corrected for the precession of the equinoxes.

To draw it, the real distance to the Sun is compressed with the dial's scale (see Planetary orbits) and the angle is shifted by 180° so that the Earth lies where the clock puts the Sun (the Sun's longitude and the Earth's differ by 180° seen from each body). The drawn Earth orbit is a closed curve of 180 points.

Solar system

Planetary orbits

The orbits of the eight planets are drawn with the same method as the Earth's: the ellipse is calculated from its orbital elements, 180 points per planet, and each point is transferred to the dial. The difficulty is scale: Neptune is about 30 AU from the Sun and Mercury 0.39. With a linear scale, if the Earth were at 175 from the center, Neptune would be at about 5,300, eleven times farther than the edge of the dial (480).

The clock uses a power scale: the radius grows as the distance raised to an exponent n less than 1, which compresses large distances. The exponent is chosen so that the Earth falls at 175 (the radius of its orbit on the clock) and Neptune's aphelion, 30.33 AU, exactly at the edge (480). This way no orbit goes off the dial and all can be told apart. Because of that compression, distances on the dial are not proportional: they serve to order and compare positions, not to measure.

Solar system

Asteroids

The main asteroid belt, between Mars and Jupiter, is represented with 170 rocks. They are not real or cataloged asteroids: they are bodies generated at random with a fixed seed, so that the belt looks the same on each load. They spread their distance between 2.1 and 3.3 AU, with more density toward the middle of the strip (average of two draws), sizes from 1.3 to 3.4 units and two colors.

Each rock does obey a real physical law, Kepler's third law: the period of an orbit grows as its semi-axis raised to 3/2. With it, how far it has advanced in its orbit (circular, with an inclination between 0° and 15°) on the simulated date is calculated. It also spins on itself, at an invented speed.

Solar system

Foreground planets

The view button changes the projection of the solar system: from the top-down XY view (looking from the ecliptic's north pole) to a side XZ view, where the plane of the orbits is seen edge-on and all the orbits lie almost on a line. Here the height above the plane is z.

In the side view the third coordinate, y, is depth: bodies with y > 0 are on the observer's side, that is, between him and the Sun, and are drawn over the solar disk; those with y < 0 are behind and are covered by it. For that the clock has an extra layer, “foreground planets”, which is painted after the Sun.

To place a point in this view, the 3D position is scaled by the same factor that compresses the distance (the power scale) and projected onto x and z, discarding depth.

Rotation

How the rotating Earth and its tilted axis are drawn.

Rotation

Representation of rotation

When the Rotation focus is chosen, the clock shows a terrestrial globe. It is the Earth as someone located on the Sun would see it: the meridian that passes right below the Sun, that of the subsolar point, lies at the center of the disk, and the globe rotates with the simulated time, so speeding up time speeds up the rotation.

The image is an orthographic projection: the sphere is viewed from very far away, and each point of the surface is flattened onto the plane perpendicular to the line of sight. The parallels are straight lines (the axis lies in the screen plane) and the meridians, half-ellipses whose width is the sine of their angular distance from the central meridian. A place is seen only if it is on the side facing the observer. The whole set is tilted 23.44° (see Rotation axis).

To know which meridian lies at the center, the Sun's right ascension and Greenwich sidereal time are used: the central meridian has longitude λ0 = α☉ − GMST. If the user's location is known, a dot marks it on the globe with its coordinates.

Numerical example

12 October 2026, 16:00 UTC: α☉ = 197.85°, GMST = 261.24°. λ0 = 197.85 − 261.24 = −63.39°: the Sun is over the meridian 63.4° west. San Salvador (φ = 13.69° N, λ = −89.19°): t = −89.19 + 63.39 = −25.80°. Visibility: cos 13.69°·cos(−25.80°) = 0.875 > 0, it is seen. Before the tilt: x = 600 + 596·cos 13.69°·sin(−25.80°) ≈ 348.0, y = 600 − 596·sin 13.69° ≈ 458.9.

The globe's axis is always drawn parallel to the screen plane: the Sun's tilt over the equator (the declination, −7.57° that day) is not applied. The point at the center is not the real subsolar one, but that of the same meridian on the equator. See Scope.

Drawing measurements: radius 596 (somewhat larger than the dial, 480), one meridian every 15° (one per time zone, 24 in all) and parallels every 30° up to ±60°, plus the tropics and the polar circles.

Rotation

Rotation axis

The Earth rotates on an axis tilted 23.44° from the perpendicular to the plane of its orbit. On the clock, the axis is a dotted line that crosses the center and extends 1000 units on each side, with an arc between the vertical and the axis showing the angle. That tilt is the cause of the seasons and of the existence of the tropics (at ±23.44° latitude) and the polar circles (at ±66.56°).

At the other end of the axis the place's rotation speed is written: how fast its surface moves as the Earth rotates. The speed at the equator is the length of the circumference (2π times the equatorial radius) divided by the time it takes to rotate, the sidereal day (23 h 56 min, the real time of one turn relative to the stars). At another latitude, the circle a point travels is smaller in proportion to the cosine of that latitude.

Numerical example

Speed at the equator: 2π·6,378,137 m ÷ 86,164.1 s = 465.1 m/s. San Salvador (φ = 13.69° N): 465.1·cos 13.69° = 451.9 m/s. At 60° latitude: 232.6 m/s, half. At the poles, 0. Polar circles: 90° − 23.44° = 66.56°.

The tilt is shown as a fixed value of 23.44°; in reality it decreases about 0.013° per century and oscillates slightly. The speed is only that of rotation about the axis: it does not include the Earth's motion around the Sun (about 29.8 km/s) nor the Sun's in the galaxy.
