The limits of precision

Models, uncertainty and scope of the astronomical calculations.

The scope of our calculations

AD ASTRA combines mathematical and astronomical models to represent the motion of the celestial bodies and to calculate various celestial phenomena. The reliability of each result depends on the method used, the data available and the approximations inherent in the calculation.

This section documents the models used, their known precision, the sources of uncertainty and the limits of each process. The goal is to offer a transparent view of the project's capabilities and to provide the context needed to interpret its results.

Knowing the limitations of a model is a fundamental part of understanding its results.

01 — Fundamentals

Precision, error and uncertainty

To interpret an astronomical result correctly, it is necessary to distinguish between the difference from a reference value, the uncertainty associated with the result, and the level of detail with which it is represented.

Error

Difference between a calculated result and a reference value, expressed in an appropriate unit.

Uncertainty

Estimate of the doubt associated with a result, taking into account the data, models and approximations used.

Resolution

Level of detail with which the system expresses or represents a result.

A fundamental distinction

Showing more decimals does not guarantee that a result is more precise.

02 — Methodology

Calculation models

AD ASTRA uses two levels of calculation. The low-precision ones are compact formulas, enough to draw the clock and show general readings. The high-precision ones are used where an error of minutes or degrees would be visible: phases, events and eclipses. Neither claims to replace an official ephemeris.

04 — Calculation conditions

Time and geographic limits

The results depend on the interval covered by each model, on the coordinates and on how time is handled. The date selector does not prevent choosing any year, but outside these intervals reliability decreases.

Time interval

High precision: designed for 1900–2150, with good measured agreement up to 2300. Before ~1890 the error grows quickly (≈ 18′ for the Moon in 1850 and several degrees around 1800) because ΔT is extrapolated. Planets: valid between 1800 and 2050; before 1800 the error reaches tens of degrees for Mercury.

Time (ΔT)

After 2050, ΔT is a prediction, not a measurement. An uncertainty of 100 s shifts the path of an eclipse by some tens of kilometers and its local time proportionally.

Time zone

With a real location, the device's time zone is used, with daylight saving time. With a manual location it is estimated from longitude (lon ÷ 15, rounded), without daylight saving time or political time zones.

Sunrise and sunset

The crossing at −0.833° at sea level is calculated. It does not include terrain, observer altitude or real refraction; the observed time can differ by minutes, especially at high latitudes, and in the polar region the state is reported (midnight Sun or polar night).

Position of the Moon

The altitude is geocentric: it does not correct for parallax or refraction.

Best place to observe

It considers the geometry and phase of the Moon, not cloud cover or light pollution.

What has not been validated

Comets and meteor showers (approximate view), magnitude and path of eclipses, the full Chinese calendar, local times of the contacts of an eclipse, the lunar outlines and the accuracy of the land map.

  • The asteroid belt is decorative.
  • Comet passage dates were entered by hand and checked against external sources in October 2026 (e.g. NASA Science, 2P/Encke). The brightness and timing of a comet are uncertain.
  • The orientation and altitude of the Moon use the low-precision model, with the differences indicated above.
  • Geocoding depends on an external service (Nominatim) and may fail or be slow.
